# Agentic & AI interface brief — 2026-10-22

**Theme: Don't let the agent be its own witness.** Today's best work keeps showing the same failure from different angles: a judge that reads the agent's success report gets talked into passing it, a monitor panel that shares one evidence source fails together, and an agent that is great at finishing tasks almost never saves the state it's about to destroy. The fix each time is to build independence into the harness: separate the claim from the evidence, count independent sources instead of votes, and make preservation the harness's job rather than the agent's.

1. **[Who Verifies the Agent's Success Claim? Self-Report Leakage in GUI-Agent Completion Judging](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29609)** (arXiv 2610.29609), the agent's "Done" shouldn't be evidence
   Accepted to the ICSE 2027 research track (Nanjing University / TUM). The same judge reads the same task and trace, and the only change is whether the agent's own report is included. Including it suppresses detection of confident-but-false success claims: trace-only recall drops by 43.4 points with Qwen3.5-35B and 13.3 with DeepSeek, and the flips are almost all in the harmful direction (79 vs 1). The fix is structural. One component reads only the report and decides what's being claimed. A second reads only the task and trace and fills typed, cited evidence fields (action, target, transition, committed, final state), and is not allowed to give a verdict. Deterministic code then maps that to pass, flag, or review. Because evidence is cached separately from policy, you can replay it under a stricter or looser policy without new model calls. Gating on flag plus review blocks 74–90% of false success claims. For your harness: the agent's summary and the verification panel should be separate objects, and the verifier shouldn't see the summary. Show per-obligation evidence ("committed? no, step 14") instead of a single checkmark, and let the user change the policy and watch verdicts update. Credibility: medium-high. Peer-reviewed venue, held-out batch, solid inter-rater agreement (κ≈0.79); Android GUI traces rather than code. Weight: high.

2. **[Back Up Before You Regret: Measuring Backup Awareness and Automating Backup in Coding Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28934)** (arXiv 2610.28934), finishing the task isn't keeping it reversible
   From Bo Li's and Weiyan Shi's groups. RegretBench has 205 executable tasks built from real "I wish I'd kept that" posts on X, GitHub and Stack Overflow. Across six frontier models, agents complete 86.1% of tasks but back up before the regret moment in only 15.2%. BackUpper is a harness plug-in that checks every tool call and saves the affected state before it changes: rules for common operations, a small subagent for odd cases, and a rule table that grows from the subagent's decisions. It covers every regret-moment action, restores state at 92.2% similarity, costs about $0.014 per task, and uses 7x fewer tokens than asking the main agent to back itself up. It can also narrow to the user's backup preferences (84.5% F1 against human labels). For your harness: reversibility belongs to the harness, not the agent's judgment. Each destructive step in the timeline should carry a snapshot you can restore with one click, plus a visible, editable "what we preserve" policy. This complements yesterday's Recoverability paper (2610.27465), which found that saved copies restore behavior and regenerated undos don't. Credibility: medium-high. Strong groups and an executable benchmark; regret tasks are curated from social posts, and "92% similarity" isn't exact restore. Weight: high.

3. **[Correlated Failures of AI Approval Monitors](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29178)** (arXiv 2610.29178), count sources, not votes
   Borrows distributed-systems "fail-prone sets" for approval panels: monitors that share a base model or read the same poisoned input fail together, so N-of-M voting overstates safety. On payment approval with twelve small open monitors (two pre-registered protocols) plus three closed frontier models, every reliable trick that got a fraud approved came in through one evidence source and fooled only the monitors that read it. A rule that counts independent sources instead of votes blocked 0.9% of correct payments vs 7.4% for the best vote-counting rule, at equal safety. The pre-registered worst case still failed, since one attack left the approvers relying on a single source. Adding a rule engine that can't be persuaded fixed that on the measured attacks. For your harness: if you stack reviewers or classifiers before an irreversible step, show which evidence each one actually read, and make the approval rule about independent sources with at least one deterministic check, not "3 of 4 models said OK." Credibility: medium. Single author, payment domain only, but honest about its pre-registered failure. Weight: medium-high.

4. **[PAUSE: Search-Free Steering of Coding Agents Before Their First Source-Code Edit](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29870)** (arXiv 2610.29870), the first edit is the natural checkpoint
   Agents reach their first source edit at a median 60% of the way through a run, after exploration, reproduction and testing. PAUSE stops that first edit before it runs. A separate Planner reads the task and the transcript so far and writes one repair plan, and the same agent then continues with its context and workspace intact. That's one intervention, with no monitor and no search. It adds +16.8% resolved on SWE-bench Verified in OpenHands and +14.7% on Multilingual in SWE-agent. It beats a rule-triggered advisor and roughly matches a periodic overseer at 11–15% of its planner cost. Offline, it rescues 35.4% of eligible failed runs from a recorded prefix with 33–41% fewer tokens than restarting. For your harness: "about to make the first write" is a cheap, well-defined moment for a plan-review card, whether a human or a second model reviews it, and resuming from a recorded prefix is a real "retry from here with a new plan" feature, not a full restart. Credibility: medium. Benchmark-only, and gains are shown with a weaker Executor (Qwen3-coder) and a stronger Planner. Weight: medium-high.

5. **[Claude Code v2.1.307](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-code/releases/tag/v2.1.307)**, supervision events become first-class telemetry
   Shipped Oct 21. A new `claude_code.safety_event` OpenTelemetry event fires for every tool call that a permission rule, auto mode, a safety check, a hook or the sandbox stopped, prompted or sent back. That's exactly the feed a fleet dashboard needs. `/permissions` > Recently denied now shows the actual denied command, and approving a row names the command, not its description. Auto mode's classifier also used to ignore an approval from there on retry, which is now fixed. HTTP MCP tool calls were being sent twice when a connection dropped; now only read-only or idempotent tools are retried, so retry policy depends on side effects. Fork subagents launched together are now told which launch is theirs, so they stop picking up each other's work. A background command stopped by a connected app is no longer reported as stopped by the user. Code Review gains `@claude review stop`. v2.1.308 is "bug fixes" only. Credibility: high (primary release notes). Weight: medium.

6. **[Preference Is Not Permission: Personalizing the Auto-Purchase Line in Agentic Commerce](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29216)** (arXiv 2610.29216), autonomy limits are per-person and learnable
   Treats "how far may the agent commit?" as a three-way output (don't, confirm first, go ahead) that depends on how complete the evidence is, reversibility, cost against the person's norm, and urgency, not just fit. Across 16 models, agents commit even when their own evidence flag says the evidence is missing. Fifteen annotators disagree about where the line sits, but in predictable ways: a simple learner on 30–40 of a person's own labels predicts their held-out line at a median 0.79, beating every prompt and model setup tested. It doesn't transfer across domains. Pairs with yesterday's AuthorizationBench. For your harness: the approve/auto boundary should be a per-user, per-domain policy learned from their actual approve/deny clicks and shown as an editable rule, not one global autonomy slider. Credibility: medium. Commerce domain, role-played principals, and the data is only promised after acceptance. Weight: medium.

**Takeaway for a non-chat+diff harness:** Keep the agent's account of its work out of the verifier's input, and show verification as cited evidence for each obligation under a policy you can replay. Put an automatic snapshot behind every destructive step instead of trusting the agent to back things up. When you stack monitors, show which evidence each one read and count independent sources, with at least one check that can't be argued with. Treat "about to make the first edit" as the plan-review checkpoint, and resume from that point rather than restarting. Learn each user's approve/auto line from their own clicks, and stream every block, prompt and denial as events a fleet view can show.
