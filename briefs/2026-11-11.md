# Agentic & AI interface brief — 2026-11-11

**Theme: The evidence was there; nothing used it.** In most failed agent runs, the trajectory already held something that contradicted the error. Memory lessons get cut off from the runs they came from. AGENTS.md rules get followed or ignored with no signal either way, and pass/fail benchmarks throw away the number that would separate two agents. For a non-chat harness, the job isn't collecting more evidence. It's putting the evidence you already have in front of the agent, the checker, and the human at the moment they decide.

1. **[Dependable Long-Horizon LLM Agents: Where Errors Enter and Which Controls Reach Them](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13254)** (arXiv 2611.13254), contradicting evidence sat unused in most failures
   The authors read 95 failed multi-agent runs (Who&When, MAD) in full and traced each fault from origin to consequence. Faults enter early, at a median of 18% of the way through the run, mostly as wrong decisions or fabricated task state. In 37 of 88 Who&When runs, the fault came before the step the human labels call decisive. In 72% of failed runs the trajectory already contained evidence against the error, and in 88% of those no agent acted on it. Something flagged a deviation in 31% of runs, but no response removed the bad state. Also, 45% of the hand-crafted Who&When failures ended in a provider content-filter crash, after which the harness emitted a final answer no agent produced. For your UI: show where a fault entered, not just where it surfaced; put unused contradicting evidence next to the claim; and never let the harness write a "final answer" for a crashed agent. Credibility: medium-high (careful full-trajectory annotation with protocol and scripts released; 95 runs on public corpora; affiliations not checked). Weight: high.

2. **ACP session options: [RFD #2791](https://github.com/agentclientprotocol/agent-client-protocol/pull/2791) / [#2792](https://github.com/agentclientprotocol/agent-client-protocol/pull/2792) / [#2794](https://github.com/agentclientprotocol/agent-client-protocol/pull/2794)**, configure a session before it exists
   Merged yesterday. `session/options` returns the config options, modes (v1), and advisory commands (v2) a session *would* start with, for a given cwd, without creating one. Pass a `sessionId` to preview a session before restoring or forking it. Setup requests (new/load/resume, and now fork) accept initial `configOptions`, applied in order. Unknown or refused values are ignored rather than failing, and the response reports what the session actually got. Other notifications wait until the response. This is the protocol hook for a real "launch" surface: pick model, mode, and permissions before the first turn, and diff requested against granted. #2794 came from implementing it in the Claude adapter. Also opened: [#2796](https://github.com/agentclientprotocol/agent-client-protocol/pull/2796), a draft OAuth RFD for remote agents (reconnect without logging in again, modeled on MCP's auth spec). It's unchampioned, so watch it but don't build on it. Credibility: high (merged in the official repo; marked unstable). Weight: high.

3. **[Do They Do as They Are Told? Coding Agents' Adherence to Explicit Instructions in Context Files](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13709)** (arXiv 2611.13709), AGENTS.md rules are either always followed or never
   The authors turned real AGENTS.md-style instructions into executable checks and scored the agent commits linked to them. Agents follow context-file guidance more often than not, but adherence splits sharply: some instructions are followed consistently and others are never respected, and the split varies by instruction category and by agent. This is the third piece after the Nov 4 policy-wording and Nov 9 stale-instruction items. Treat each context-file rule as a checkable contract with a pass/fail light, and move "never followed" rule types out of prose into enforcement. Credibility: medium (empirical mining study; exact compliance rates are in the paper, not the abstract; affiliations not checked). Weight: medium-high.

4. **[Don't Just Trust Your Memory: Evidence-Linked Memory Reduces Memory-Induced Degradation in LLM Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.14320)** (arXiv 2611.14320), every lesson keeps its receipt
   Memory makes agents fail tasks they would otherwise solve, because lessons are stored without the trajectories they came from and can't be checked. WikiTrace links each insight to a why-it-worked/failed summary and to the raw trajectory. The agent drills down when a lesson lacks detail, conflicts with another, or came from a failure. It beats other memory methods across five agentic benchmarks and reduces the degradation. Design cue for a harness memory panel: each remembered rule carries a link to its source run, an outcome badge, and a conflict marker. Credibility: medium (five benchmarks, code released; academic group, affiliations not checked). Weight: medium.

5. **[From Questions to Decisions: How LLM Agents Acquire Decision-Relevant Information from Humans (Ask2Decide)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13015)** (arXiv 2611.13015), humans don't answer the question you needed answered
   Ask2Decide has 43 planning tasks where a solver labels which hidden facts actually change the optimal plan. Initial plans were infeasible on 58–98% of tasks when information was missing (vs 5–28% with everything provided). Agents found plan-changing facts barely more often than irrelevant ones (39–52% vs 32–41%). From 79 sessions with 19 real people: after a wrong plan, human replies left out the correcting fact 43% of the time, versus 7% for a naive simulated user. Pairs with the Nov 2 shared-attention question queue. Questions should be tied to the decision they unlock, and the UI should show which assumption a plan rests on, so the human can spot the missing fact. Credibility: medium (small human study, labeled ongoing work; solver-grounded relevance labels are a real strength). Weight: medium.

6. **[Agent Benchmarks Stop Ranking Before They Run Out of Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13541)** (arXiv 2611.13541), saturation is a measurement artifact
   Re-scoring released runs from eight benchmarks: neighboring top-ten agents on SWE-bench Verified differ by less than the tests' own error. In a Terminal-Bench re-run, models tied on pass/fail differed more than tenfold on the quantity the tests discard. The proposed fix is graded credit with a clipped log-ratio score, dated anchors, and cost as a separate axis. Extends the Nov 4 silent-instrument-errors item. For your own evals and fleet dashboards, report the graded quantity and cost per run, not a pass/fail bit. [ExpertCrowd-SWE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13246) (arXiv 2611.13246) does this in practice: 100 expert-written tasks with partial-credit graders and lazy/adversarial control patches. Credibility: medium-high (re-analysis of public released runs; methodology-heavy). Weight: medium.

**Also noted:** [No finite rulebook can certify every safe program](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13367) (arXiv 2611.13367) proves that a code-only gatekeeper can catch violations by watching but can never permanently clear a "never do X" or confidentiality rule. Approval UIs should say "allowed so far, monitored," not "certified safe." I skipped ActiveSecHarness (2611.13960): interesting capability-token and taint-lattice design, but it was evaluated on self-built fixtures against a 4B judge. Claude Code v2.1.322 (Vertex fast mode only) and Codex 0.167.1 (no notes) had nothing interface-relevant.

**Takeaway for a non-chat+diff harness:** Surface contradicting evidence at the moment of the claim, and show where a fault entered. Make every memory lesson and context-file rule link to its source and its compliance check. Add a pre-session launch panel that diffs requested config against granted. Tie each question to the decision it unblocks. Score runs on graded quantity plus cost, not a pass/fail bit.
