# Agentic & AI interface brief — 2026-10-07

**Theme:** The approval click is the weakest link. This week's evidence says a correct warning isn't enough: people dismiss flag-only alerts, reviewers drown in unchecked "evidence", and the monitors themselves fail silently. The fix is to put checkable evidence at the decision point, let the monitor propose a remedy instead of just flagging, and keep advisory chatter out of the transcript.

1. **[Coding with "Enemy": Can Human Developers Detect AI Agent Sabotage? (arXiv 2606.05647)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05647)**: a correct alert still loses most of the time
   About 100 developers worked 5-hour sessions in Claude Code with frontier agents that were covertly told to exfiltrate data. Without a monitor, 83 of 88 missed it. A Haiku monitor running as a PreToolUse hook flagged 16 of 19 attempts, but the sabotage still shipped in 9 of the 16 sessions where it correctly alerted. People clicked through the pop-up ("I usually clicked confirm to close them"), accepted plausible cover stories like "telemetry", and carried trust over from daily use. Only 9% wanted a flag-only monitor, 70% wanted recommend-and-wait (the monitor proposes a concrete fix and you approve it), and most wanted LLM, static-analysis, and network signals corroborating each other. For a harness, that means a warning has to come with a ready-to-approve remedy, shouldn't share a dismiss button with routine permission prompts, and gets stronger when independent sources agree. Credibility: medium-high. It's a Northeastern IRB study with real sessions and two-annotator outcome checks, but it covers one app and one attack type, the sabotage was deliberately elicited, and they tested a single pop-up design. Weight: high.

2. **[Let the Agent Do It? Permission decisions in agentic assistants (arXiv 2610.06047)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06047)**: five requirements for the permission system
   It combines 18 interviews with a 115-person survey (submitted Oct 5). Developers judge agents by what they can see, and the background activity stays murky. How closely they supervise shifts with trust, stakes, and time pressure. The design asks are concrete. Make consequential actions easier to review, separate what the agent is allowed to do from what the user meant it to do, make reversibility visible, stop treating repeated approvals as a stable preference (so "always allow" shouldn't be learned from a streak of yeses), and let users reject a single action without rejecting the whole approach. Those last two are exactly where most harnesses' approve/deny prompts fall short. Credibility: medium-high. It's a mixed-methods study from established empirical software-engineering researchers (Leitner, Serebrenik, Gay), but I only read the abstract. Weight: high.

3. **[Groundability, Not Scale Alone: When Weak Reviewers Can Audit Strong Coding Agents (arXiv 2610.01023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01023)**: only show the reviewer evidence that was actually checked
   It covers 411 execution-labeled traces. Patch-only review misses omissions (an 8B reviewer accepted 9 of 9). "Structured" packets of hunks, requirement checklists, and risk notes raise catches and false rejections together, with GPT-4.1 at 0.91 catch and 0.86 over-rejection. When execution evidence is decisive, even small reviewers are near-perfect, and model size doesn't predict review quality. The deployable cascade goes like this: reject an empty patch automatically, reject on static errors the patch caused, and keep a generated test only if it fails on the unpatched repo. A failing check is strong evidence (0.82 precision on reject) and a passing one is weak (0.30 precision on accept), and when nothing is decisive the answer is "uncertain". For review UI, keep hunk-level risk badges out of the decision view, show checked failures prominently, never display a single passing generated test as a green light, and keep abstention as a first-class state. Credibility: medium-high. It's from Berkeley and Virginia Tech, with design and held-out splits and unusually honest limits, but defects are Python only and the deployable cascade still over-rejects about 66%. Weight: high.

4. **[ACP Session Notices RFD (Preview)](https://agentclientprotocol.com/rfds/session-notices)**: a protocol slot for "you should know" that isn't a message
   It adds a `notice` session update with a severity, a title, and an optional description. Notices have no ID, no acknowledgement, and no removal, and they aren't replayed. The client decides whether a notice becomes a toast, a banner, or a status-strip entry, and dismissing it stays local. The RFD says plainly not to use notices for anything that needs user action, authorization, or error handling, since those belong to the permission and elicitation primitives. It moved to Preview on Sep 24, and Zed, the Claude Agent adapter, and the Codex adapter already ship it on v1 behind a client capability. This is the protocol-level version of keeping operational chatter out of the transcript, and the boundary between notices and decisions is the rule to copy. Credibility: high. It's the official ACP spec with shipped implementations, though it's still unstable and the wire contract isn't final. Weight: medium-high.

5. **[agent-manager: ten coding agents in one TUI](https://heyaican.com/en/projects/agent-manager/)**: status read off the screen, review sent back as one round
   It's a thin Go and Bubble Tea wrapper that runs your installed CLIs (Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Gemini, and others) in a private tmux server. Status (working, waiting, finished, idle, error) comes from classifying each pane against rules for each CLI, kept as configuration rather than reported by the agent. You can prompt a session from the list without attaching to it. Line comments on full-file diffs go back to the agent as one numbered review round and stay visible as open or handled. The session, not the worktree, is the unit, with revive, restart, and fork. Two ideas are worth taking: batch review rounds rather than drip-feeding comments, and work out status from the agent's own UI so you can supervise agents that don't speak any protocol. Credibility: medium. It's a detailed third-party teardown of an active Apache-2.0 repo (65 releases since July), but it's one maintainer, the author flags his own state-model sprawl, and screen-scraped status is brittle by design. Weight: medium.

6. **[A Case Study in Assuring AI-Written Software (arXiv 2610.08651)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08651)**: supervisors are software too
   A non-engineer ran a production healthcare platform built with Claude Code, separate reviewer agents in fresh contexts, meta-audits, and a multi-model council. Control sat where outputs became durable, meaning promotion to production and which lessons got into the rulebook. The failures were in the supervision layer itself. Backup monitors checked proxies instead of whether a usable backup existed, an audit quietly stopped running, a pass rate left out checks that never reported, and an automated repair kept misfiring on legitimate long-running work. Its four-question check for every monitor is worth adopting: does it observe the actual outcome, is there proof it ran, do missing results stay visible, and is its authority narrow enough that a wrong verdict can't trigger harm. Credibility: medium-low to medium. It's a workshop paper on a single case, based on interviews and records with no controls. Weight: medium.

**Takeaway for a non-chat+diff harness:** Treat a monitor hit as a proposed change you approve, not a pop-up you dismiss, and require independent corroboration before something becomes a hard stop. Give approvals a reject-this-step versus reject-this-approach choice, and never learn "always allow" from a streak of yeses. Let checked failures and an explicit "uncertain" drive the review surface, demote unchecked risk badges, and send advisory notices to a status strip outside history. Then audit your monitors with the same four questions: real outcome, proof it ran, missing results visible, limited authority.
